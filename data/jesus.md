# The Resurrection of Jesus

> *It is strongly encouraged that before reading this section you first read the sections on the existence of God in general, as it is impossible to grant a resurrection of Jesus without believing a God exists.*

## I. Setup

This argument proceeds in two steps: first, establishing which facts about Jesus' death and its aftermath are historically secure; and second, determining which explanation best accounts for the cause of those facts. Doing it in this order keeps the argument honest. The facts are settled before any hypothesis is in view, and every hypothesis, including the resurrection, must then explain the same facts.

### A. The Historical Data

The facts below are drawn from what most critical New Testament scholars accept, including many who reject the resurrection. This matters for the argument's strength: it does not assume the gospels are inerrant, or even generally reliable. It treats the sources the way historians treat any ancient text, and uses only what survives that scrutiny.

Each fact is stated, supported, and paired with its main point of dispute.

#### 1. Jesus died by Roman crucifixion.

This is among the best-attested facts of ancient history. It appears in all four gospels and throughout Paul's letters, and it is independently reported by two non-Christian historians: the Jewish historian Josephus (*Antiquities* 18.3) and the Roman historian Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44). The early church also had every reason *not* to invent it. A crucified messiah was a scandal to Jews and absurd to Romans, as Paul himself acknowledges (1 Corinthians 1:23).

*Dispute:* Virtually none among scholars. The swoon hypothesis (Section II.A) is the only challenge, and it is addressed there.

#### 2. Jesus was buried in a known tomb.

The burial is part of the earliest tradition we have: the creed Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15 states that Jesus "was buried." But burial alone is not enough for the argument. Under normal Roman practice, crucifixion victims were often left on the cross or thrown into a common grave for criminals. For Jesus to have been placed in a known, identifiable tomb, someone with standing had to break that pattern by asking Pilate for the body. Mark's account, generally regarded as early, names that person: Joseph of Arimathea, a respected member of the council, who requested the body and laid it in a rock-cut tomb (Mark 15:42–46).

If Joseph's role is historical, three things follow: the burial was in a specific, private tomb rather than an unmarked grave; its location was known, at least to Joseph and likely to the council he belonged to; and the tomb could be checked. If the body had remained there, it could have been produced.

Several features support this account:

- **Joseph of Arimathea is unlikely to be an invention.** A critic might argue that early Christians invented him to give Jesus an honorable burial, perhaps to fulfill Isaiah 53:9, which says the servant would be with a rich man in his death. But several details point the other way:
  - **A Sanhedrin member is an awkward hero.** Early Christians held the council responsible for Jesus' death, which makes one of its members an unlikely figure to invent as the man who honored him.
  - **Arimathea is an obscure town** with no symbolic meaning, the kind of detail that reads as memory rather than invention.
  - **Mark's account is plain.** It cites no prophecy and adds no embellishment. The description of Joseph as rich, the detail that echoes Isaiah, appears only in Matthew (27:57), the later gospel.
- **The women saw where he was buried.** Mark, Matthew, and Luke all report that women followers watched the burial and saw where the body was laid (Mark 15:47; Matthew 27:61; Luke 23:55). When they returned after the Sabbath to anoint the body (Mark 16:1), they were going back to a tomb they had seen two days earlier, which John places in a garden near the site of the crucifixion (John 19:41–42). Several features support the women's presence:
  - **Women were unlikely witnesses to invent.** In first-century Jewish and Roman society, women's testimony carried little weight; Josephus records that women were not accepted as witnesses in Jewish law (*Antiquities* 4.219). The embarrassment was real: in the second century, the pagan critic Celsus mocked the resurrection story as resting on the word of a hysterical woman (reported in Origen, *Against Celsus* 2.55). An author inventing witnesses would have chosen men.
  - **The male disciples look bad by comparison.** Mark reports that the men all fled when Jesus was arrested (Mark 14:50), while the women stayed through the crucifixion and burial. A story shaped to honor the church's leaders would not make them the ones who ran.
  - **The women are named.** Mark names Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses, and Salome in the next scene. Identifying Mary through her sons suggests they were known to Mark's readers. As Richard Bauckham argues, named figures like these likely served as the known sources behind the accounts, people the early communities could ask. Inventing named witnesses who could be checked would have been risky.
  - **Their presence is consistent across the accounts.** All four gospels place women at the cross, and Mary Magdalene appears in every list. Their return to anoint the body also fits Jewish custom, in which women prepared the dead.
- **It fits Jewish practice.** Jewish law required burial of the executed before sundown (Deuteronomy 21:22–23), and Josephus notes that Jews buried crucified victims accordingly (*Jewish War* 4.317).
- **Archaeology shows it was possible.** The ossuary of Yehohanan, a crucified man discovered near Jerusalem in 1968, shows that at least some crucifixion victims received proper tomb burial.

*Objection: What if the women at the burial were a narrative device to set up their later visit?*

A critic may argue that Mark needed the women to know where the tomb was so they could find it empty, and that their watching the burial is a literary bridge between the two scenes rather than a historical memory. Three considerations weigh against this:

- **There was no objection for the detail to answer.** No one in Mark's time was claiming the women went to the wrong tomb. The earliest counter-story was that the disciples stole the body (Matthew 28:11–15), which assumes everyone knew which tomb was meant. An author is unlikely to invent a detail to defend against a charge no one was making.
- **The detail follows from the visit itself.** If the women went to the tomb to anoint the body, they must have known where it was, whether or not Mark narrated how they learned. And the location was known independently of them, to Joseph, who owned the tomb.
- **An inventor would have chosen different witnesses.** As noted above, women were the least credible witnesses an author could have picked. Someone constructing a bridge between scenes would have used figures whose testimony carried weight.

The honest limit is that this detail rests mainly on a single source, Mark, since Matthew and Luke draw on his account. It is best stated as strong evidence, not proof.

#### 3. The tomb was found empty.

- **The proclamation began in Jerusalem.** The disciples preached the resurrection in the city where Jesus was buried, within weeks of his death. A body in a known tomb would have ended the movement immediately.
  - **Paul's letters confirm the Jerusalem setting.** Paul reports that about three years after his conversion, he went to Jerusalem to meet Peter and also saw James (Galatians 1:18–19), and that on a later visit he laid out his message before the Jerusalem leaders, naming James, Peter, and John, who approved it (Galatians 2:1–10). He also says that before his conversion he persecuted the church, and that the churches of Judea knew him as their former persecutor (Galatians 1:13, 22–23). Together, these show a community centered in Jerusalem, led by Jesus' closest followers and proclaiming the resurrection, within the first few years after the crucifixion. Acts 2 aligns with this, placing Peter's first public proclamation of the resurrection in Jerusalem at Pentecost, fifty days after the crucifixion.
    - **Why Paul's letters are reliable.** Galatians and 1 Corinthians are among the letters that virtually all scholars, including skeptical ones, accept as written by Paul himself. Paul was a highly trained Pharisee and a zealous opponent of the early church, who by his own account tried to destroy it (Philippians 3:5–6; Galatians 1:13–14). He held a promising position among the Jews: he describes himself as advancing in Judaism beyond many of his peers (Galatians 1:14), and Acts adds that he studied under Gamaliel, a leading teacher of the law, and carried authority from the high priest to arrest Christians (Acts 22:3; 9:1–2). He gave up that standing to become the movement's most active missionary, at the cost of beatings, imprisonment, and repeated danger (2 Corinthians 11:23–27), and he was eventually executed in Rome for his faith, as reported by Clement of Rome around AD 95 (*1 Clement* 5). A man who switched sides at such a cost had little to gain from misrepresenting the Jerusalem church. And he wrote while Peter and James were alive and able to contradict him.
- **The earliest opposition conceded it.** Matthew 28:11–15 reports that Jewish authorities claimed the disciples stole the body, and the same charge appears in the second century (Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 108). The polemic disputes *why* the tomb was empty, not *whether* it was.
- **Women are named as the discoverers.** Women's testimony carried little legal weight in first-century Jewish society. An invented account would more likely have featured male disciples, so the detail is best explained by its being what happened.

*Dispute:* This is the most contested fact in the data set. Paul never explicitly mentions the empty tomb, and Mark's gospel ends abruptly with the women fleeing. Gary Habermas's survey of the scholarly literature reported that roughly three-quarters of scholars accept it, but that leaves a significant minority, and the survey's method has been criticized. The argument should be shown to hold even if this fact is set aside.

#### 4. Individuals and groups had experiences they took to be appearances of the risen Jesus.

- **The 1 Corinthians 15 creed.** The key source is the creed in 1 Corinthians 15:3–8, which lists appearances to Peter, to the Twelve, to more than five hundred at once, to James, to all the apostles, and finally to Paul himself. Paul says he "received" this tradition, most likely during his visit to Jerusalem about three years after his conversion, when he met Peter and James (Galatians 1:18–19). That places the creed within a few years of the crucifixion: far too early for legend to have replaced the core memory.
  - **It names living, checkable witnesses.** The creed names Peter and James, and Paul adds that most of the 500 were still alive when he wrote (1 Corinthians 15:6), essentially inviting his readers to go ask them. Someone inventing a story usually avoids pointing to people who could expose it. That is suggestive, but not decisive, since a group of liars could all tell the same story.
  - **The key witnesses suffered and died for it.** This is the main evidence that they sincerely believed what they claimed, and it is strongest for exactly the people named in the creed:
    - **Peter:** executed in Rome, reported by Clement of Rome around AD 95 (*1 Clement* 5).
    - **James, the brother of Jesus:** executed in Jerusalem around AD 62, reported by the Jewish historian Josephus (*Antiquities* 20.200), a non-Christian source.
    - **Paul:** executed in Rome, also reported in *1 Clement* 5.
- **Even skeptical scholars accept this fact.** Gerd Lüdemann and Bart Ehrman, for example, both grant that the disciples had experiences they understood as seeing Jesus alive.

*Dispute:* The fact establishes that the experiences occurred, not what they were. Whether they were physical encounters, visions, or something else is the central question of Sections II and III.

#### 5. Former skeptics and opponents converted.

- **James**, the brother of Jesus, did not believe in him during his ministry (Mark 3:21; John 7:5). Afterward he became the leader of the Jerusalem church (Galatians 1:19; Acts 15) and was executed for his faith around AD 62, as reported by Josephus (*Antiquities* 20.200). The creed lists an appearance to him.
- **Paul** was an active persecutor of the church (Galatians 1:13; Philippians 3:6). He became its most influential missionary because of what he took to be an appearance of the risen Jesus.

*Dispute:* Converts' testimony shows a change of belief, not its cause. Critics argue Paul's conversion could have psychological explanations, and that James's conversion is reported too briefly to analyze.

#### 6. The disciples proclaimed the resurrection early and suffered for it.

The resurrection was the center of the earliest Christian preaching, not a later addition. Paul's letters describe repeated beatings, imprisonment, and danger endured for the message (2 Corinthians 11:23–27). The martyrdoms of Peter and Paul are reported by Clement of Rome around AD 95 (*1 Clement* 5).

*Dispute:* The evidence for most individual apostles' martyrdoms is weak or late; the general willingness to suffer is what is well attested. Willingness to suffer also shows sincerity, not truth. It rules out deliberate fraud but not honest error.

#### What these facts do and do not show

None of the six facts above is, by itself, evidence for God or for a miracle. They are historical claims about what happened and what people believed: that Jesus died and was buried, that his tomb was found empty, that his followers and some former opponents sincerely believed they had seen him alive, and that they proclaimed this at great cost. A skeptic can accept every one of them and still deny the resurrection, explaining the facts by natural causes. That is precisely why they make a fair starting point: they are the shared data that both sides must explain. Whether they point to a resurrection, and so to God's action, is a separate question, taken up in Sections II and III.

## II. The Natural Alternatives

This section examines the main natural explanations that have been proposed for the facts in Section I. Its role is defensive. The case for the resurrection rests on its own evidence, set out in Section III; the purpose here is to show that no natural rival accounts for that evidence as well. Each hypothesis is presented in its strongest form and judged by the criteria in Section IV.A: what it explains, where it fails, what its defenders say in reply, and how that reply fares.

### A. Swoon: Jesus survived crucifixion and recovered

**The hypothesis.** Jesus did not die on the cross. He lost consciousness, was mistaken for dead, and was placed in the tomb, where the cool air revived him. He then left the tomb and showed himself to his followers, who concluded he had risen. Versions of this view were popular among German rationalists in the early nineteenth century, and it has occasionally been revived since, for example in Hugh Schonfield's *The Passover Plot* (1965).

**What it explains.** The empty tomb and the appearances: the tomb was empty because Jesus walked out of it, and the disciples saw him because he was alive.

**Where it fails.**

- **Crucifixion was among the most brutal forms of execution ever devised, and the Romans were experts at it.** It was designed not only to kill but to make death as slow, painful, and public as possible, and it was reserved for slaves, rebels, and the lowest criminals. The Roman orator Cicero called it the cruelest and most hideous of punishments (*Against Verres* 2.5.165), and our word "excruciating" comes from the Latin for the torment of the cross.
  - **Scourging came first.** Victims were typically flogged beforehand, as Mark reports of Jesus (Mark 15:15), with a whip of leather straps often weighted with pieces of bone or metal. Scourging could tear open the back down to the muscle and alone was sometimes fatal.
  - **The cross itself killed slowly.** Victims were nailed or tied to the cross; the heel bone of Yehohanan, the crucified man found near Jerusalem, still has an iron nail driven through it. Hanging by the arms made breathing increasingly difficult, and death came through some combination of exhaustion, blood loss, shock, and suffocation, sometimes over many hours. The precise medical cause is still debated, but the outcome was not in doubt.
  - **The executioners did this routinely.** Rome crucified on a massive scale: some six thousand followers of the slave rebel Spartacus were crucified along the Appian Way in 71 BC, and Josephus reports that during the siege of Jerusalem in AD 70, the Romans crucified as many as five hundred people a day (*Jewish War* 5.451). The soldiers who carried out these sentences knew when a man was dead.
  - **Jesus' death was confirmed.** John reports that a soldier pierced Jesus' side with a spear to make sure of his death, producing a flow of blood and water (John 19:34). Mark reports that Pilate confirmed the death with the centurion before releasing the body (Mark 15:44–45).
- **Survival without care is implausible.** After scourging and crucifixion, Jesus would have been gravely injured. He would have had to survive more than a day in a sealed tomb without food, water, or treatment, free himself from the burial wrappings, and move the stone.

**The defender's reply.** Crucifixion victims sometimes lived for days, and Mark reports that Pilate was surprised Jesus had died so quickly (Mark 15:44), which might suggest he had not. Josephus also records that he once persuaded the Roman commander Titus to take down three of his acquaintances from crosses, and that one of them survived with medical care (Josephus, *Life* 75).

**Response.** Josephus's case shows the opposite of what the swoon theory needs. His acquaintances were taken down deliberately, while still alive, and given the best available treatment, yet two of the three died anyway. Jesus was taken down because he was judged dead, wrapped for burial, and left without care. Pilate's surprise led him to check, and the centurion confirmed the death. Even if survival were somehow granted, the theory would still fail to explain the central fact: the disciples' belief that Jesus had been raised in glory.

### B. Theft or fraud

**The hypothesis.** The body was removed from the tomb by human hands. In one version, the disciples stole it and then falsely proclaimed that Jesus had risen. In another, someone else moved it: Joseph, the authorities, or grave robbers, and the disciples, finding the tomb empty, drew the wrong conclusion.

**What it explains.** The empty tomb.

**Where it fails.**

- **If the disciples stole the body, they were lying, and the evidence shows they were not.** As Section I showed (facts 4 and 6), the key witnesses suffered and died for their claim, gained nothing worldly from it, and never recanted. People may die for a falsehood they believe, but it is hard to see a whole group of leaders enduring persecution and death for a story they knew they had invented.
- **Anyone who moved the body could have produced it.** If Joseph or the authorities had moved it, they knew where it was, and the authorities, at least, had every reason to end the movement by showing it.
- **If someone else moved the body, the appearances and conversions remain unexplained.** An empty tomb might produce confusion or grief, but not the conviction that Jesus had appeared to Peter, the Twelve, more than five hundred people, James, and Paul. Nor would a missing body have turned a skeptic and a persecutor into believers: James and Paul were not at the tomb (fact 5).

**The defender's reply.** The earliest Jewish response was exactly this: Matthew reports that the authorities claimed the disciples had stolen the body while the guards slept (Matthew 28:11–15). Some also suggest that Joseph's tomb was only a temporary resting place, chosen because it was near and the Sabbath was approaching (John 19:42), and that the body was later moved to a permanent grave without the disciples knowing.

**Response.** The early theft charge confirms that the tomb was empty, but it does not make theft likely: it was the explanation the opponents of the movement needed, not evidence of what happened. The temporary-tomb version fails for the same reason as the others. Joseph would have known where he moved the body, and the disciples' proclamation in Jerusalem would have given him reason to say so. Neither version explains the appearances or the conversions.

### C. Hallucinations and grief visions

This is the strongest natural alternative, and it receives the most attention here.

**The hypothesis.** The disciples, devastated by Jesus' death, experienced visions of him. Such experiences are well documented: studies of bereaved people have found that many report seeing, hearing, or sensing a loved one after death. Peter, perhaps overwhelmed by grief and guilt at having denied Jesus, had the first such vision, and his conviction spread to the others, producing further experiences. Paul's experience, in this view, arose from inner conflict over his persecution of the church. The hypothesis has been developed in detail by Gerd Lüdemann and Michael Goulder, and Dale Allison, who is not himself a naturalist, has argued that bereavement visions must be taken seriously as part of the picture.

**What it explains.** The appearance experiences (fact 4), especially those of individual, grieving followers, and the sincerity of the disciples' belief (fact 6). It is the only natural hypothesis that explains both why the disciples believed they saw Jesus and why they were not lying.

**Where it fails.**

- **Visions would not have been called resurrection.** In first-century Jewish thought, seeing a dead person in a vision meant seeing a ghost or a spirit. "Resurrection" meant something specific: the raising of a dead body, expected for all the righteous at the end of time. If the disciples had seen visions, they had ready categories for them: Jesus' spirit was with God, or he had been exalted to heaven. The claim that he had been raised from the dead, as an individual, in the middle of history, was a new and unexpected interpretation, and visions alone do not account for it.
- **It does not explain the empty tomb.** If the body was still in a known tomb, visions would not have survived the obvious check (facts 2 and 3). The hypothesis therefore needs an additional explanation for the tomb, which leads to the combination hypotheses below.
- **Group appearances strain the hypothesis.** The creed lists appearances to groups: the Twelve, more than five hundred at once, and all the apostles (1 Corinthians 15:5–7). These are in the earliest source, not in later embellishments. Hallucinations are typically private experiences, which makes a single vision shared by many people harder to explain than an individual one. But this is not decisive. Collective experiences are reported in other settings, usually among groups under strong emotion or shared expectation, and the creed gives no description of what each group actually saw. It is possible that these were shared experiences of some kind, interpreted together. The group appearances therefore count against the hypothesis without ruling it out.
- **The converts were not grieving.** Bereavement visions arise from grief and longing. James did not believe in his brother during his ministry, and Paul was actively persecuting Jesus' followers. Neither fits the profile of a mourner who sees the person they long for (fact 5).

**The defender's reply.** Collective visionary experiences are reported in other settings, such as crowds at Marian apparitions, where groups claim to have seen the same thing. Strong expectation and social contagion can lead people to see, or believe they have seen, what others report. The group appearances in the creed may also describe something like this: a shared experience, interpreted together, rather than many people seeing an identical figure.

**Response.** The collective visions offered as parallels usually occur in crowds already expecting a sign, and the participants commonly report different things. The disciples had no such expectation: they had fled, and they had no category for one man rising before the end of time. The defender's reply also concedes that the hypothesis cannot stand on its own. To account for the group appearances, it must reinterpret the creed; to account for Paul and James, it needs separate explanations of a different kind; and to account for the tomb, it must reject facts 2 and 3. Each concession adds to the hypothesis's cost.

**Honest limit.** The creed lists the group appearances but does not describe them. That leaves room for interpretation, and it is the main reason the vision hypothesis remains the most serious natural rival.

### D. Legend: the story grew over decades

**The hypothesis.** The resurrection accounts developed over time. Whatever the earliest believers experienced, the stories were expanded, dramatized, and made more physical as they were retold, until the gospels, written decades later, presented a bodily risen Jesus who ate, spoke, and was touched.

**What it explains.** The differences between the gospel accounts and the increasingly physical details of the later ones, such as Jesus eating fish (Luke 24:42–43) and inviting Thomas to touch his wounds (John 20:27).

**Where it fails.**

- **The core claim is too early.** The creed dates to within a few years of the crucifixion (fact 4). The historian A. N. Sherwin-White, studying how ancient historical memory was transmitted, argued that even two generations were too short a time for legend to wipe out a historical core. Here the core claim, that Jesus was raised and appeared to named people, is attested within a few years.
- **The witnesses were still alive.** Paul knew Peter and James personally, and he wrote while they and many others could contradict him. Legend grows most easily when no one remains to correct it.
- **Legend needs something to grow from.** At most, legendary development explains how a story was embellished. It does not explain why the disciples came to believe Jesus had been raised in the first place.

**The defender's reply.** The early creed lists appearances but gives no details about them. The physical features of the later accounts may well be legendary, developed partly to answer the charge that the disciples had merely seen a ghost. Some scholars, such as Crossan and Ehrman, also regard the burial in Joseph's tomb as a later development.

**Response.** The argument can grant that some narrative details may have developed over time without losing anything essential. The facts in Section I do not depend on the physical details of the later gospel accounts. Legend can explain the shape of the stories; it cannot explain the facts beneath them. The burial is defended separately in fact 2.

### E. Combination hypotheses

**The hypothesis.** No single natural explanation covers all the facts, but several together might. A typical combination: Jesus' body was placed in an unknown or common grave, so there was no tomb to check; Peter, grieving, had a vision, which spread to the others through shared expectation; James and Paul had visions of their own for separate reasons; and over time, legend added the empty tomb story and the physical details of the appearances.

**What it explains.** In principle, all six facts, by assigning a different natural cause to each.

**Where it fails.**

- **It must reject facts 2 and 3.** The combination works only if the known burial and the empty tomb are dismissed as later legend, against the evidence presented in Section I.
- **Each added piece costs probability.** Every component is an additional assumption, and the probability of the whole is lower than the probability of any single part. This is exactly what the criterion of being less ad hoc measures (Section IV.A). A hypothesis built from many separate causes, each introduced to cover a single fact, is weaker for that reason.
- **It still struggles with the converts and the group appearances.** The combination must posit an independent vision experienced by a skeptic and another by a persecutor, as well as collective experiences in the early creed, without the grief and expectation that make visions plausible elsewhere.

**The defender's reply.** Unlikely combinations of ordinary events happen all the time, and any combination of natural causes, however improbable, is still more likely than a miracle. A historian, on this view, should prefer any natural explanation to a supernatural one.

**Response.** This reply is the most important objection to the whole argument, but it is not a historical point. It does not dispute the evidence; it claims that the resurrection should be ruled out regardless of the evidence. That claim depends on a prior judgment about whether God exists and acts, and it is addressed directly in Section III.D and III.E. Judged by the historical criteria alone, a combination hypothesis has broad scope only because it is assembled to fit, and it pays for that scope in plausibility and simplicity.

### F. Jesus never existed

A small number of writers, such as Richard Carrier and Robert Price, argue that Jesus was a mythical figure who never existed as a historical person. This view is rejected by nearly all historians, including skeptical ones; Bart Ehrman wrote a book-length rebuttal, *Did Jesus Exist?* (2012). It contradicts fact 1, which is attested by Paul, who met Jesus' brother, and by the non-Christian historians Josephus and Tacitus. It is noted here only for completeness.

## III. The Resurrection Hypothesis

**The hypothesis.** God raised Jesus from the dead. His body did not remain in the tomb; it was raised and transformed, and he appeared to his followers and to others over a period of time.

This section sets out the positive case. The resurrection is not accepted because its rivals fail, but because of how well it accounts for the evidence. It is judged by the same four criteria applied to every other hypothesis in Section II.

### A. Explanatory scope

The resurrection accounts for all six facts in Section I, and it does so without rejecting any of them:

- **Death (fact 1):** the hypothesis presupposes it. Jesus really died.
- **Burial in a known tomb (fact 2):** his body was laid in Joseph's tomb, as reported.
- **Empty tomb (fact 3):** the tomb was empty because the body had been raised.
- **Appearances (fact 4):** individuals and groups experienced appearances because Jesus actually appeared to them.
- **Converts (fact 5):** James and Paul came to believe because Jesus appeared to them as well, whatever their prior attitude.
- **Proclamation and suffering (fact 6):** the disciples proclaimed the resurrection at great cost because they were convinced, on the basis of what they had seen, that it was true.

No natural alternative in Section II covers all six. Each one explains some facts and must either reject or separately explain the rest. The combination hypotheses come closest, but only by assembling a different cause for each fact and by rejecting facts 2 and 3.

### B. Explanatory power

The resurrection does more than make the facts possible; it makes them what we would expect.

- **It explains why the disciples spoke of *resurrection*.** As Section II.C noted, Jews of the time had ready categories for seeing someone after death: a ghost, a spirit, or a righteous person exalted to heaven. None of these required an empty tomb, and none was called resurrection. The historian N. T. Wright argues that neither an empty tomb alone nor appearances alone would have produced the belief: an empty tomb alone suggests theft, and appearances alone suggest a ghost or a vision. Together, they produce exactly the claim the disciples made. The resurrection hypothesis supplies both.
- **It explains the group appearances.** If Jesus was physically present, it is no surprise that several people at once saw him. This is the fact the vision hypothesis struggles with most.
- **It explains the converts without relying on grief.** An appearance by someone who is really there does not depend on the emotional state of the person who sees him. A skeptical brother and an active persecutor are as likely to see him as a grieving follower.
- **It explains the speed and confidence of the proclamation.** The disciples went from flight to public proclamation in Jerusalem within a short time, and they held to it under persecution. That is what we would expect from people who were convinced they had met Jesus alive.
  - **At the crucifixion, the disciples gave up.** When Jesus was arrested, the men all fled (Mark 14:50), and Peter, their leader, denied three times that he even knew him (Mark 14:66–72). John describes them afterward meeting behind locked doors for fear of the authorities (John 20:19), and Luke has two followers on the road to Emmaus saying that they *had hoped* Jesus was the one to redeem Israel (Luke 24:21), speaking of that hope as something already lost. John's gospel even has several of them returning to their old work as fishermen in Galilee (John 21:3).
  - **They had good reason to.** In Jewish expectation, the Messiah was to triumph over Israel's enemies, not be executed by them. A crucified man was, by the words of the law, under God's curse (Deuteronomy 21:23; cited by Paul in Galatians 3:13). To his followers, the crucifixion was not a setback but a verdict: Jesus had failed, and the movement was over.
  - **Then everything changed.** After the appearances, the same men, including Peter, who had denied Jesus, were proclaiming him publicly in the city where he had been executed, before the same authorities they had feared, and they continued to do so through imprisonment, beatings, and eventually death. A group that had scattered in despair became, in a short time, united and bold.
  - **This is the turnaround any explanation must account for.** These details are awkward for the gospel writers, who had no reason to invent the cowardice and despair of the church's own leaders, which makes them likely to be historical. Something happened between the crucifixion and the proclamation that changed the disciples completely. The resurrection supplies a cause equal to that change: they were convinced, because they had seen him, that Jesus had been vindicated by God.

### C. Not ad hoc

A hypothesis is ad hoc when it adds unsupported assumptions to rescue itself. The resurrection requires one major assumption: that God exists and is able and willing to act in history. That assumption is addressed in Section III.D. Beyond it, the hypothesis needs no further additions, and it does not arise from nowhere. It occurs in a specific context that gives it meaning:

- **Jesus' ministry.** Jesus claimed unusual authority, to speak for God and to forgive sins. In his lifetime he was widely regarded as a prophet (Mark 6:15; 8:28) and was known above all as a healer and exorcist, someone through whom extraordinary things happened.
  - **Even his opponents conceded the deeds.** Strikingly, the ancient sources hostile to Jesus do not deny that he did remarkable things; they dispute where his power came from. In Mark, the scribes from Jerusalem accuse him of casting out demons by the power of Beelzebul, the prince of demons (Mark 3:22), an accusation the early church would hardly have invented. The Jewish historian Josephus, who was not a Christian, describes Jesus as a doer of surprising or wondrous deeds (*Antiquities* 18.63). Later Jewish tradition preserved in the Babylonian Talmud says that a man named Yeshu was executed for practicing sorcery and leading Israel astray (*Sanhedrin* 43a), and the second-century pagan critic Celsus claimed Jesus had learned magic in Egypt (reported in Origen, *Against Celsus* 1.28). Sorcery and magic were the standard charges against someone whose deeds could not be denied but whose message was rejected.
  - **Modern scholars accept the reputation.** Many critical scholars, including some who doubt the miracles themselves, accept that Jesus was known in his lifetime as a healer and exorcist. E. P. Sanders counts it among the most secure facts about him, and John P. Meier devotes a large part of his historical study of Jesus to the question.
  - **What this does and does not show.** The agreement of friends and opponents shows that Jesus had a reputation for extraordinary deeds from the beginning, not one invented later by his followers. It does not by itself show that his powers were supernatural: his opponents attributed them to dark forces, and modern skeptics explain them naturally, for example as psychosomatic healings in a culture that expected them. Its value for this argument is contextual. A resurrection would not be the first extraordinary event associated with Jesus, but the culmination of a life already marked by them.
- **His predictions.** The gospels report that Jesus predicted both his death and his vindication (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34). The authenticity of these sayings is debated, since they could have been shaped after the fact, but the expectation that God would vindicate a righteous sufferer was already part of Jewish thought.
- **The meaning of the event.** A resurrection in this context is not a random marvel. It is God's answer to the question Jesus' death posed: whether his claims were true. That is why the earliest believers took it as confirmation that Jesus was who he said he was.

If God exists, this is the kind of situation in which one might expect him to act. That is what keeps the hypothesis from being ad hoc.

### D. Plausibility

Plausibility is where the resurrection pays its price. On a purely naturalistic view of the world, in which nothing exists beyond nature, a resurrection is about as implausible as anything could be, and almost any natural explanation, however strained, would be preferable.

But that judgment depends on the background beliefs one brings to the question, and those are not settled by history. The earlier sections of this project argue on independent grounds that God exists, and the plausibility of this argument rests heavily on the success of the earlier sections of this site. If those arguments succeed, a world created and sustained by God is one in which God can act, and the laws of nature describe what happens when nothing beyond nature intervenes, not what must happen in every case. The resurrection does not claim that nature raised Jesus, but that God did.

Given theism, the remaining question is whether there is reason to think God would raise *this* man. Section III.C supplies that reason: Jesus' claims, his reputation, and the expectation of vindication give the event a context in which it makes sense.

This means the conclusion of this section is tied to the strength of the earlier ones. Someone who rejects theism on independent grounds will reasonably remain unpersuaded by the historical case alone, and the argument does not pretend otherwise. But for someone who accepts the earlier arguments, or who is at least open to God's existence, the resurrection is not implausible from the start, and the historical evidence can be weighed on its merits.

The relationship runs in both directions. Theism raises the plausibility of the resurrection; and if the resurrection is the best explanation of the evidence, it is in turn significant evidence for theism. This is not circular, provided each direction is used at the right point: the earlier arguments are developed independently of the resurrection, and the resurrection is then added to the cumulative case as a further, independent line of evidence.

### E. Objections

#### 1. A miracle is never the most probable explanation

**The objection.** David Hume argued that a miracle is a violation of the laws of nature, and that the uniform experience that establishes those laws is stronger than any testimony to the contrary. On this view, it will always be more likely that the witnesses were mistaken or deceived than that a miracle occurred. Bart Ehrman makes a related point about method: historians can only establish what probably happened, and a miracle is by definition the least probable of all events, so historians as historians can never conclude that one occurred.

**Response.**

- **Hume's argument assumes its conclusion.** Experience is uniform against resurrections only if no resurrection has ever occurred, which is the very question at issue.
- **Improbable events are established by evidence all the time.** The prior improbability of an event does not settle whether it happened; what matters is how much more likely the evidence is if the event occurred than if it did not. The philosopher of science John Earman, who is not a theist, argued in *Hume's Abject Failure* (2000) that Hume's argument fails on exactly this point.
- **The probability of a miracle depends on whether God exists.** A resurrection is not improbable in itself, but improbable given naturalism. If God exists, the question becomes whether God acted, and that is a question the evidence can help answer.
- **Ehrman's point concerns method, not truth.** It may be that historical method alone cannot reach a conclusion about God's action. But that leaves open whether the total evidence, historical and philosophical together, supports the resurrection, which is the question this project asks.

#### 2. There may be a natural explanation we haven't thought of

**The objection.** Even if every known natural explanation fails, some unknown one might succeed. History is full of puzzles later solved naturally.

**Response.** This is always possible, and it is one reason the conclusion is stated as the best explanation rather than as proof. But an unspecified explanation cannot be weighed. It has no scope, no power, and no plausibility that can be assessed, because it has no content. If the bare possibility of an unknown explanation were enough to block a conclusion, it would block every historical inference. The reasonable course is to judge the explanations actually available.

#### 3. Other religions also report miracles

**The objection.** Many religious traditions report miracles, including figures said to have healed, appeared after death, or ascended to heaven. If we accept this miracle claim, consistency seems to require accepting theirs, and if we reject theirs, consistency seems to require rejecting this one.

**Response.** The argument does not claim that all miracle reports are false, nor that all are true. It claims that this one should be judged on its evidence, and that every other claim should be judged the same way. Few other miracle claims have evidence of comparable quality: a written source within a few years of the event, named and living witnesses, the conversion of skeptics and opponents, and witnesses who suffered for their claim. The miracles attributed to the first-century sage Apollonius of Tyana, for example, are known mainly from a biography written by Philostratus more than a century after his death. The consistency the objection asks for is fair, and it cuts both ways: a miracle claim elsewhere with evidence of similar strength would deserve similar consideration.

#### 4. Paul describes a "spiritual body"

**The objection.** Paul writes that the body "is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44), and that "flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God" (15:50). Critics argue this shows that the earliest Christian understanding of the resurrection was not bodily, and that the physical appearance stories of the gospels are later developments.

**Response.**

- **"Spiritual" describes what animates the body, not what it is made of.** The Greek word Paul uses, *pneumatikos*, is contrasted with *psychikos*, usually translated "natural," not with "physical." Paul uses the same contrast elsewhere to describe a "spiritual person" (1 Corinthians 2:14–15), meaning someone led by God's Spirit, not someone without a body.
- **It is still a body.** Paul's word is *sōma*, body, and his analogy is a seed that is sown and then raised as a plant (15:36–38): the same thing, transformed, not replaced. Elsewhere he writes that Christ will transform our lowly bodies to be like his glorious body (Philippians 3:21).
- **"Flesh and blood" is an idiom.** In Jewish usage, it refers to ordinary mortal human nature, not to physical matter as such. Paul uses the phrase this way when he says that after his conversion, he did not consult with "flesh and blood," meaning other people (Galatians 1:16).

**Honest limit.** Paul does stress that the resurrection body is transformed, not simply restored. That is consistent with the gospel accounts, in which the risen Jesus is physical but also appears and disappears and is sometimes not at first recognized. What the evidence supports is a raised, transformed body, not a resuscitated corpse.

## IV. Conclusion

### A. Criteria for Judging Explanations

Historians judge competing explanations of the past by how well they account for the evidence. The criteria below are adapted from the historian C. Behan McCullagh (*Justifying Historical Descriptions*, 1984):

1. **Explanatory scope:** How many of the facts does the hypothesis account for? A hypothesis that explains the appearances but not the empty tomb has narrower scope than one that explains both.
2. **Explanatory power:** How well does it account for them? A strong explanation makes the facts what we would expect; a weak one merely leaves them possible.
3. **Plausibility:** How well does it fit what we already know about the world? This is where background beliefs enter. A hypothesis is more plausible the more it is implied by things we have independent reason to accept.
4. **Less ad hoc:** How few new, unsupported assumptions does it require? Every assumption added to rescue a hypothesis lowers its probability.

Two notes on applying them:

- **They apply equally to every hypothesis.** The resurrection receives no exemption. It must show greater scope and power than its rivals and must answer the challenge of plausibility.
- **Plausibility is where the cumulative case matters.** A naturalist and a theist will assess the plausibility of a resurrection very differently, because they begin with different background beliefs. The earlier sections of this project establish theism on independent grounds. If they succeed, the resurrection cannot be dismissed as implausible from the outset, and the question becomes a straightforward historical one: which hypothesis best explains the data?

### B. Comparative summary

The table below summarizes how each hypothesis fares against the four criteria above. Fact numbers refer to the data set in Section I.A.

| Hypothesis | Scope | Power | Plausibility | Ad hoc |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Swoon** | Explains the empty tomb and appearances; contradicts the death (1) | Weak: the facts follow only if Jesus survived, which the evidence makes very unlikely | Very low, given Roman crucifixion | High: requires survival without care and an escape from the tomb |
| **Theft or fraud** | Explains the empty tomb (3) only | Weak: leaves appearances and conversions unexplained | Low: contradicts the witnesses' sincerity (6) | Moderate: needs further causes for the remaining facts |
| **Hallucinations** | Explains individual appearances (4) and sincerity (6); strained for converts (5); none for the tomb (2–3) | Moderate for individuals; weak for groups and the resurrection claim itself | Visions are common, but less so for groups and non-grieving converts | Moderate: requires a separate explanation for the tomb |
| **Legend** | Explains later narrative details; none of the core facts | Weak: needs something to grow from | Low, given the early creed and living witnesses | Moderate |
| **Combination** | Covers all six, but only by rejecting facts 2 and 3 | Moderate: a different cause for each fact | Each part is possible; the whole is much less likely | High: assembled to fit the data |
| **Resurrection** | All six facts, rejecting none | Strong: makes the facts what we would expect | Very low on naturalism; reasonable given theism | Low: one assumption, that God exists and acts |

Two things stand out. First, the resurrection is the only hypothesis that accounts for every fact without rejecting any, and the only one whose explanatory power is strong across the whole data set. Second, its one weakness is plausibility, and that weakness depends entirely on whether God exists. Every natural hypothesis has weaknesses in scope or power that remain whatever one's background beliefs.

### C. The conclusion

The historical evidence is best explained by the resurrection of Jesus.

This conclusion is stated as the best explanation, not as proof. History does not deliver certainty about any ancient event, and the argument has acknowledged its limits throughout: the burial and empty tomb rest mainly on one early source; the group appearances are listed in the creed but not described; and an unknown natural explanation can never be ruled out entirely.

The conclusion's strength also depends on what comes before it. For someone convinced on independent grounds that there is no God, a resurrection will remain less plausible than any natural explanation, however strained. For someone who accepts the arguments of the earlier sections, or who is at least open to God's existence, the obstacle disappears, and the question becomes an ordinary historical one: which hypothesis best explains the data? On that question, the resurrection has no serious rival.
